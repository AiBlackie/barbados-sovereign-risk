1. Tiered Decomposition of the BDS $9.81B Figure

The register in Section 3 aggregates items of materially different maturity, valuation basis, and certainty. This methodological note decomposes them into three tiers. The decomposition strengthens the finding: Tier 1 alone — BDS $2.0B of contracted debt stock — sits outside the IMF's DSA perimeter, independent of any actuarial assumption.

3. Two Caveats on Tier 3

NIS ($4.2B). This figure derives from the 17th Actuarial Review (2020), the most recent publicly available review. The IMF's 2026 Article IV (p.60, Figure 6) notes that 2022–23 parametric reforms extended the fund's lifespan, measuring a residual annual financing need of 0.2–0.4 pp of GDP rather than a stock shortfall. The updated actuarial review is not public; the $4.2B therefore remains the only verifiable stock figure, but should be read as pre-reform. Post-reform, the stock is likely lower.

Government Pension ($2.16B EST). This is a 2.5× annual payroll actuarial proxy pending the Ministry of Finance's pension study. It is a conservative floor, not a point estimate, and is flagged accordingly in the original register.

4. The Reserve Stress-Test: Full Derivation

The 24.1-month reserve projection in the Executive Alert of the original brief is a scenario calculation, not a forecast. Its inputs and arithmetic are set out in full below so that any reviewer can replicate or vary them without requesting underlying material.

The arithmetic. The buffer of ~BDS $1.62B divided by the target timeline of 24.1 months yields an implied net monthly drain of ~BDS $67M. This is the average rate at which reserves would need to fall to hit the 12-week benchmark in exactly 24.1 months. It is important to note that this is a derived figure for the stress-test timeline, not the same as the current-account-based annual burn rate shown in the dashboard sidebar. The sidebar burn rate (BDS $665.75M/year) reflects the current account deficit plus estimated debt service minus FDI. The stress-test drain (BDS $67M/month, or ~BDS $804M/year) reflects the specific pace required to exhaust the reserve buffer to the 12-week benchmark. The two figures are related but measure different things. The stress-test number is higher because it includes the drawdown of the buffer above the benchmark, not just the annual external gap.

What the test does and does not claim. It does not claim that reserves will reach the benchmark in 24.1 months. It claims that if FDI fell to zero and external debt service continued in full, the buffer would be exhausted on that timeline. The IMF's 2026 Article IV assesses reserves as "ample" under its central baseline — a baseline that assumes FDI continues. The two statements are not in conflict: the IMF describes the central case, and this test describes the tail. The value of the test is that it quantifies how much of the "ample" assessment depends on FDI continuing to arrive.

5. Post-Publication Development: The November 2026 Debt-for-Resilience Swap

On November 18, 2026 — one day after the publication of the original brief — Barbados announced the terms of a landmark debt-for-resilience swap. The transaction is the first under the Multi-Guarantor Debt for Resilience Joint Initiative, coordinated by the Inter-American Development Bank and backed by the IDB, the World Bank Group, CAF, and the Caribbean Development Bank. The brief did not address this development because it had not yet occurred. It is addressed here because it bears directly on the analysis.

Transaction mechanics. Barbados will buy back US$600 million of the bonds issued in its 2018 debt restructuring. These will be refinanced with new 20-year loans at an average rate of 3.25%, against the 7%–8% carried by the retired bonds. The interest saving is earmarked to fund US$160 million in health and resilience investment over the life of the loans, including a partial rebuild of the main hospital in Bridgetown. The four development banks are providing guarantees totalling US$600 million (IDB and World Bank US$175 million each; CAF and CDB US$125 million each). Scotiabank and CIBC Caribbean acted as lead arrangers. The transaction has been reported as the largest debt-for-health swap on record; this characterisation is attributed to press reporting and is not independently verified here. Sources: Reuters / MarketScreener (November 18, 2026); Inter-American Development Bank announcement, November 18, 2026.
Open item — buyback price. Public disclosures to date do not state whether the US$600 million of 2018 bonds were repurchased at par or at a discount. This matters for the analysis below. If the buyback was executed at par, the swap is principal-neutral and the conclusions in this section hold as written. If it was executed at a discount, the swap reduces principal by the discount amount, and the Tier 2 exposure is marginally smaller than stated. This addendum does not assert either outcome. Reviewers with access to the final transaction documents should confirm the buyback price before drawing conclusions about principal reduction.

What the swap does not do. On the terms disclosed to date, the swap is a liability management operation, not a debt cancellation. It retires US$600 million of high-coupon debt and replaces it with lower-coupon debt of the same notional amount. It improves the maturity and interest profile of the reported debt stock. It does not address the exposures identified in Sections 1–3 of this addendum. Specifically:

  • The CBB negative equity (Tier 2) is untouched. The 2018 restructuring that created the BDS $1.42 billion negative equity on the Central Bank's balance sheet is the same restructuring whose bonds are being retired. The swap refinances those bonds; it does not recapitalize the Central Bank. The IMF has documented that Barbados' recapitalization plan involves profit retention followed by a gradual predetermined payment plan (IMF 2026 Article IV, p.25, ¶27). That plan remains unfunded at current profit levels. The swap's success in lowering debt service costs may, if anything, reduce near-term pressure to accelerate that recapitalization.
  • The NIS and Government Pension exposures (Tier 3) are untouched. The swap generates fiscal space for health investment, not for pension consolidation. The BDS $4.2 billion NIS actuarial shortfall and the unfunded Government Pension Plan remain outside the perimeter.
  • The swap is another LMO, not a perimeter expansion. The original brief analyzed the June 2025 Eurobond LMO in Section 2.G: a debt-neutral operation that extended maturities without changing the stock of unconsolidated liabilities. The November 2026 swap follows the same pattern. It is a sophisticated, well-structured transaction that improves Barbados' reported debt dynamics. It does not bring the Tier 2 or Tier 3 exposures onto the balance sheet.

What the transaction does confirm. The transaction is genuinely positive for Barbados on one dimension: it lowers the coupon on US$600 million of debt from 7–8% to 3.25%. On the disclosed terms, that implies a gross annual interest cost reduction of roughly US$22–28 million while the full balance is outstanding, before guarantee fees and before amortization reduces the principal. This is a calculated implication, not an official disclosure; the World Bank and IDB announcements describe the transaction as generating "fiscal savings" without specifying the amount. The transaction also demonstrates that Barbados retains access to multilateral guarantee capacity. The IMF's June 2026 Article IV had already assessed Barbados' debt as sustainable and on a downward path, reaching 96.0% of GDP at end-FY2025/26 on the Fund's fiscal-year GDP basis (equivalent to 94.6% on the calendar-year basis used in this brief).

But it reinforces precisely the divergence the original brief identified: the reported trajectory is improving while the unconsolidated exposures remain unchanged. The more successful Barbados is at managing its reported debt profile through LMOs and guarantee-backed refinancing, the more comfortable it becomes to defer the Tier 2 and Tier 3 obligations. The transaction is evidence of sophisticated debt management. It is not evidence that the perimeter has been expanded.

The core finding is unchanged, and the November swap reinforces its logic. The official 94.6%/96.0% figure reflects a DSA perimeter choice, not a measurement of all obligations of the sovereign. The IMF's 2026 Article IV marks both the NIS and the Central Bank as "Not consolidated" in its own Debt Coverage and Disclosure table (Annex II, Figure 1, p.58). Tier 1 of this addendum — BDS $2.0B of contracted debt stock — sits outside that perimeter and is directly comparable to gross debt. Tier 2 adds BDS $1.42B of central bank negative equity. Tier 3 adds BDS $6.4B of actuarial and contingent exposure. The November 18 debt swap is a well-executed liability management operation that improves the reported profile. It does not touch Tiers 1, 2, or 3. The exposures are real, the perimeter is the issue, and the finding of the November 17 brief stands.
